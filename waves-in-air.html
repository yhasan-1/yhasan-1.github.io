Waves in Air

Godot | GDScript | Solo Project

A narrative-focused puzzle game about communication, conflict, and community.

University of Maryland Honors Humanities Keystone Project

Project Overview

This project was my submission for the University of Maryland Honors Humanities Keystone Project. We were asked to design a project that combined research and art to address an issue of our choosing.

I chose to design a project that communicated the importance of communication and allowed an audience to reflect on how miscommunication causes interpersonal conflicts and hinders the creation of a community.

I decided to create a narrative-focused puzzle game centered around communication. The player takes on the role of a courier who delivers letters and solves puzzles involving communication barriers between different people.

This was a solo project, so I was responsible for conducting research, creating concept art, writing the story, designing the gameplay, and implementing the game in Godot.

Gameplay and Design

One of my main design goals was to make communication an active part of gameplay, rather than simply a theme discussed through dialogue.

The Radio Station Puzzle

In one puzzle, the player approaches a neglected radio station to deliver a letter but is turned away by a distressed radio operator.

While exploring the surrounding environment, the player can notice signs of neglect: dried flower beds and overgrown vines suggest that the station was once carefully maintained but has since fallen into disrepair.

Rather than convincing the operator through dialogue, the player must observe the environment and consider how the operator's circumstances might be affecting communication.

By watering the flowers and clearing the vines, the player provides some comfort to the operator, who becomes more receptive and accepts the letter.

I designed this puzzle to demonstrate that communication is about more than exchanging words. Understanding another person's circumstances and responding to their needs can be just as important.

Research and Inspiration

As part of my Keystone Project, I conducted research into communication, conflict resolution, linguistics, and interactive storytelling. I wanted to better understand why miscommunication occurs and how it affects different relationships and communities.

Communication and Conflict

Raymond Cohen's research on language and conflict resolution helped me understand how different cultures can assign different meanings to seemingly equivalent concepts. These differences can create misunderstandings, even when people believe they understand each other.

I also studied Boaz Keysar's research on egocentric communication, which explores how people often assume that others share their knowledge or perspective.

Another article, "Agreeing Is Not Enough: The Constructive Role of Miscommunication," explored how recognizing and repairing misunderstandings can contribute to better collaboration.

Together, these sources helped me think about communication as more than simply transmitting information. Effective communication also requires understanding how another person interprets that information.

Game Design Inspiration

I studied several games to better understand how gameplay and storytelling can communicate ideas to players.

Chants of Sennaar and 7 Days to End with You were especially influential in my initial design. Both games use language and interpretation as core gameplay mechanics, encouraging players to derive meaning through context.

I also studied VA-11 HALL-A for its character-driven writing and portrayal of relationships affected by failures to communicate.

Dredge provided inspiration for pacing, environmental storytelling, and the use of isolation to give players moments for reflection.

These works helped me consider how narrative, gameplay mechanics, and the environment could support the themes I wanted to explore.

Development Challenges

1. Managing Scope

I ran into several challenges as I developed the game, and I learned a lot by navigating and solving them.

Firstly, I ran into issues of scope. I had originally intended the game to be a language-based puzzle game similar to Chants of Sennaar or 7 Days to End with You.

However, designing multiple languages to represent different regions while also figuring out how to implement them as puzzles proved too difficult within the three-month timeframe we were given.

To decide how to handle this, I listed the allowances and constraints that the language puzzles imposed on my game. After reviewing these, I decided that language was both too difficult to implement and wasn't necessary for the message I wanted to convey.

Upon cutting this part of the game, the final vision became much clearer.

What I learned: The importance of keeping a realistic scope and not hesitating to cut overambitious goals.

2. Event-Driven Game Progression

This was only my second or third attempt at making a game in the Godot Engine, and it was the first game I made of this type. I had mostly experimented with platformers before this project, so much of the implementation was unfamiliar to me.

One challenging aspect was implementing puzzles that progressed as the player explored and interacted with objects and characters in the world.

To implement these features, I drew on my prior experience with Scratch. I remembered that sending messages to other sprites was critical for updating the game as the player progressed.

Godot offered signals that behaved similarly, so I studied the documentation and designed a suite of custom signals to manage the player's progress.

What I learned: How to approach development from a top-down perspective and apply concepts from previous projects to unfamiliar technologies.

3. Implementing Player Navigation

Another challenge I faced was implementing player movement.

My previous games featured movement as part of the player's skill expression, but for this project I wanted movement to be simple enough that players could focus on the puzzles and narrative.

I felt that a click-to-move system best fit my design, but I had no experience implementing one.

I researched guides and documentation, then designed a small test area where I could experiment with Godot's navigation system.

I gradually introduced additional mechanics, including obstacles and interactable objects, debugging each feature as it was added.

What I learned: How to break down an unfamiliar system into manageable problems and build functionality through incremental development and testing.

4. Developing a Cohesive Narrative

Lastly, I struggled to write a strong narrative for the game.

After my first several drafts, I knew that something was missing from the story, but I couldn't figure out exactly what was wrong.

I reached out to my project mentor, who helped me recognize that although I had developed strong individual narrative elements, I was missing the overarching structure needed to connect them.

With this feedback, I reconsidered how the story was organized and rewrote the narrative around a clearer structure. This allowed me to deliver a story that I was satisfied with.

What I learned: The importance of recognizing when independent iteration isn't working, seeking another perspective, and incorporating feedback into my work.

Final Product and Reflections

The project resulted in a playable, narrative-focused puzzle game that combines exploration, environmental interactions, and communication-driven storytelling.

Throughout development, I learned how to manage a project from research and concept development through implementation, balance ambition with practical constraints, and independently learn unfamiliar tools.

More than anything, Waves in Air reinforced my interest in game development and demonstrated how interactive experiences can be used to explore meaningful ideas.
