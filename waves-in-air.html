Waves in Air

Godot | GDScript | Solo Project | Three Months

A narrative-focused puzzle game exploring how miscommunication causes conflict and hinders the creation of a community.

Overview

For my University of Maryland Honors Humanities Keystone Project, I was asked to combine research and art to address an issue of my choosing. I created a game to help players reflect on the importance of communication.

Players take on the role of a courier, delivering letters and solving puzzles involving communication barriers. As the sole developer, I handled the research, concept art, narrative, game design, and implementation.

Turning Communication into Gameplay

I wanted players to think about communication through their actions, not just dialogue.

The Radio Station Puzzle

The player approaches a neglected radio station to deliver a letter, but the distressed operator turns them away.

  • Observe: Dried flower beds and overgrown vines suggest that the station was once cared for.
  • Respond: The player waters the flowers and clears the vines to provide some comfort.
  • Resolve: The operator becomes more receptive and accepts the letter.

I designed this puzzle to show that communication also means understanding someone's circumstances and responding to their needs.

Research and Inspiration

I researched why miscommunication occurs, how it affects relationships, and how games can communicate those ideas through storytelling and mechanics.

  • Communication: Raymond Cohen's work on cultural differences in meaning, Boaz Keysar's research on egocentric communication, and research on repairing misunderstandings.
  • Game mechanics: Chants of Sennaar and 7 Days to End with You inspired my initial language-based puzzle concept.
  • Storytelling: I studied VA-11 HALL-A for its character writing and Dredge for its pacing and environmental storytelling.

Development Challenges

1. Managing Scope

  • Challenge: I originally planned several fictional languages and translation puzzles, but designing and implementing them wasn't realistic in three months.
  • Approach: I listed the allowances and constraints of the language system and realized it wasn't necessary for the message I wanted to convey.
  • What I learned: Keep a realistic scope and don't hesitate to cut overambitious goals.

2. Event-Driven Game Progression

  • Challenge: I needed puzzles to progress as players explored and interacted with the world—something unfamiliar from my earlier platformer projects.
  • Approach: I remembered Scratch's message system, researched Godot signals, and designed a suite of custom signals to manage player progress.
  • What I learned: Use prior experience to learn new tools and approach systems from the top down.

3. Building Click-to-Move Navigation

  • Challenge: Unlike my earlier games, I wanted movement to be simple so players could focus on the puzzles and narrative.
  • Approach: I studied guides, built a test area, and gradually added obstacles and interactions. I debugged navigation issues and adjusted overlapping regions and spacing.
  • What I learned: Prototype unfamiliar systems and introduce complexity one piece at a time.

4. Strengthening the Narrative

  • Challenge: After several drafts, I knew something was wrong with my story but couldn't identify what was missing.
  • Approach: My mentor pointed out that the individual elements were strong but lacked an overarching structure. I reorganized and rewrote the narrative.
  • What I learned: When independent iteration stops helping, seeking feedback can reveal what you've overlooked.

Outcome

I completed a playable puzzle game combining research, writing, art, and programming. More importantly, the project taught me to manage scope, learn unfamiliar systems, and revise my work without losing sight of its original message.

I also prepared a narrative write-up and a playable build for my Keystone submission.
